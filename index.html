Gemini Decisions API scores 1,000 questions in one pass

Google's gemini-decision-1 scores yes/no, multiple-choice and rubric questions against one multimodal input up to 256K tokens, returning probabilities and confidence in 150-250 ms end to end. Flide: shared-state inference for Jev-style decisions, not another text generator

AgentsWeb

Flide reuses one input state across multiple typed decision questions through exact state forking and serves the /v1/systemone API. Its Nov 9 Show HN follows a Nov 8 update adding AutoTrust and Clef support; the project site claims up to 9.5x faster inference, while its README says 9.4x, on favorable shared-context benchmarks.

Why it matters: A direct capstone experiment: compare latency and decision stability when many checks share one context. The authors say the largest gain is Qwen3.5-0.8B on QuALITY, roughly 5.8k tokens and 18 questions per article; single-question gains can approach a tie on larger models. These are author benchmarks, not an independent result. State forking is meant to preserve each question's isolated computation; numerical deviations from float32 still exist, and batch invariance is an optional setting.

InfrastructureWeb

Google announced two new systems around existing eighth-generation TPU silicon: 8tx pairs confidential computing with a low-latency network, while 8i Max packages two 8i chips together for double the compute, SRAM and HBM bandwidth. These are new system configurations, not a new chip generation.

HOPE prunes MoE experts by preserving their partnerships

CraftWeb

Amazon's Nov 9 engineering explainer walks through HOPE: calibrate pairwise expert interactions, solve a pruning objective, then emit a smaller checkpoint without retraining. On Qwen3.5-35B-A3B with half the experts removed, the authors report SWE-Bench Pro 42.3 versus 41.8 unpruned and 36.2 for REAP.

Why it matters: Active-parameter counts do not shrink resident weights. Pruning actually removes experts and can free memory for KV cache, but the apparent no-loss result is one configuration, not a promise across models or workloads. The underlying paper is from September; today's value is the practical walkthrough. Gains are strongest at 40-50% pruning, while low pruning rates can favor simpler baselines; calibration and downstream evals remain necessary.

WorldParticle unifies a simulation architecture, not one set of weights

ResearchWeb

Today's MIT News writeup revisits WorldParticle, a particle-transformer architecture for cloth, solids, fluids, sand and molecular dynamics. The primary paper explicitly trains one network per dynamics category: a common architecture and workflow, not a single shared-weight universal simulator.

Why it matters: The reusable idea is prediction-correction: integrate known external forces explicitly, then learn interaction residuals while merging particle tokens for global communication. The paper limits generalization to tested held-out settings, notes an approximately 50,000-particle limit on an 80GB A100, and leaves shared-weight cross-category training as future work. The May paper is older than today's news hook; we omit the press article's broad speed and accuracy framing rather than imply replacement of specialized solvers.

AlphaProof Code verifies libsignal properties down to machine code

ResearchWeb

DeepMind's Nov 10 technical paper describes AI-assisted proofs of selected PQXDH and Triple Ratchet properties in libsignal compiled to AArch64. It reports about 6.5 million lines of Lean proof code, checked against human-approved specifications, with about 26,000 lines of trusted specifications and machine semantics.

Why it matters: The engineering lesson is a hard acceptance gate: untrusted proof generation, checked against a reviewed spec by Lean's kernel. This does not prove the whole Signal app secure. The paper assumes cryptographic primitives and execution models, and its semantics exclude threads, signals and dynamic linking. Human review of what is being proved remains load-bearing. Primary paper and released artifacts were inspected, not just the announcement.

ToneBench: measure judge noise before trusting the leaderboard

CraftNewsletter

Today's Decoding AI newsletter shares Louis-François Bouchard's account of 28,200 LLM judgments: rubric errors, repeat-run variance, model-family preference and order effects can quietly move rankings. He recommends human-written criteria, blind human ratings on the same categories, exact judge IDs and repeated scoring.

Why it matters: Turn it into an eval checklist: score deterministic constraints in code; hide model identity; compare both pairwise orders; drop one judge at a time and recompute rankings. Crucially, the author says the new 240-excerpt human-rated golden set is only starting and has no agreement results yet. This is a useful engineering report, not a validated universal judge recipe or proof that a four-judge panel removes bias.

Sixteen RL methods collapse into two dials: baseline and clip

CraftNewsletter

Today's Daily Dose maps 16 policy optimization methods by the two things they actually change: what reward baseline they subtract and how they constrain off-policy ratios. It groups PPO, VAPO and VinePPO; REINFORCE variants; GRPO, Dr. GRPO, DAPO and LitePPO; then GSPO, GMPO, CISPO and SAPO. ReMax's critic-free baseline is the concrete cost example, cutting reported 7B training GPU memory by about 46%.

Why it matters: Use the map before adopting a post-training recipe: identify whether it changes credit assignment or update trust, then ask whether that fixes your actual failure. The same issue also shows why hybrid LLMs shrink KV cache: only a few attention layers grow with context while linear layers hold fixed state. The reported gains come from papers and vendors, not drop-in guarantees.

AlphaGenome 2 reads variants cell type by cell type

ResearchWeb

DeepMind's conditional DNA model takes a million-letter sequence plus a tissue, cell type or state, trained on more than 107 million individual cell profiles across 68 tissues. It predicts gene expression, histone modifications, chromatin accessibility and DNA methylation, and is available for non-commercial research through the AlphaGenome API, with commercial access in private preview.

Why it matters: The architectural move is conditioning on one biological context at a time, plus a cell encoder that can represent unseen single-cell states without retraining. DeepMind reports 30% versus 16% causal-variant recall and 43% versus 23% directional-effect prediction against the prior setup on TenK10K. Treat those as vendor results; the model is research-only and not approved for clinical or diagnostic use.

OpenAI's persistent-agent controls: split trust, sandbox everything

From productionWeb

OpenAI says more than 500 persistent OpenClaw agents now run inside the company. Its control pattern separates a trusted gateway from the untrusted agent harness; sandboxes network, filesystem, process and secrets; strips GitHub tokens; auto-reviews every action; gives each agent its own identity; and logs configuration plus turns for security review.

Why it matters: This is a concrete checklist for always-on agents: keep networking, identity and credential brokering in deterministic code, give the agent only a writable workspace, route review through a second model before spending human attention, and make every agent action attributable. OpenClaw Enterprise is still a pilot, and OpenAI's productivity claims are vendor-reported; the controls are the useful part.

Omnigraph gives multi-agent memory Git-style branches

CraftNewsletter

Omnigraph is an MIT-licensed Rust graph database for shared agent state: typed ontology, graph/vector/BM25 retrieval, object-storage-native data, per-agent branches, three-way merges, snapshots and Cedar policies enforced server-side. In Daily Dose's demo, Claude Code and Codex wrote in parallel, and Omnigraph refused the stale merge with a divergent_update.

Why it matters: Multi-agent memory fails by lost update when agents read a record, then write back a stale whole-record version. Branches turn each agent's work into a reviewable diff and stop stale merges without forcing every write through one agent. The demo is a vendor-style walkthrough; test merge granularity, policy setup and operational overhead before adopting it.

JetBrains: agent scale comes from workflow readiness, not seat count

From productionWeb

JetBrains' nine-case report contrasts Fin, which built a Team 2x around shared skills, plugins, MCP servers and evaluations, with an organization that reached 95% AI adoption without improving developer velocity. Fin reports 3x pull-request throughput, a 54% smaller defect backlog and 50% lower cost per PR over 16 months.

Why it matters: More agents do not fix a weak engineering system. Make repositories agent-ready first: standard builds, fast tests, accessible APIs, self-setup environments, shared evals and enough review capacity. Then train developers to delegate real tasks and measure team outcomes, not adoption. The figures come from a vendor-published report, but the failure pattern is concrete.
