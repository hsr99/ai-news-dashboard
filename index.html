Pick of the week

Jev vs Laya, paired and self-audited: the decision-model eval to study

A paired evaluation (Laya AI / TypeSafe AI) put open-weight Laya against hosted Jev across 11 agent decision points from 18 public sources: 7,283 base cases plus 6,640 robustness variants, byte-identical inputs, cross-hardware and cross-day reproducibility checks. Jev wins 9 of 11 (+10.8 to +46.0 points); neither beats chance on zero-shot model routing; they tie on RAG relevance gating. Then the authors audit their own pipeline: a claimed 23.9% cost saving was really 4.3%, gate accuracy had been reported as end-to-end quality (58% vs 98%), thresholds were tuned in-sample. Everything - cases, raw outputs, analysis code - is public.

Why it matters: The most capstone-relevant read of the week. The paired-test design with reproducibility checks is the evidence standard her evaluation-reliability work asks for, and the self-audit - a team publishing precisely how its own deployment claims were inflated - is the rarest thing in applied eval. Steal both: the methodology and the audit checklist for judging Jev, Laya, or your own evaluators.

Independent numbers on Jev: 3x faster and 2x cheaper than a small chat model

ModelsFrom production

Dwayo ran Jev (jev-1.13.0) against Claude Haiku 5.5 with thinking off on three public classification tasks (Banking77 at 77 and 10 classes, Aegis 2.0 content safety): median latency 346-372ms versus 1,035-1,057ms, roughly 2.1-2.5x lower list-price cost, 4.0 points ahead on 77-class Banking77, and within noise on the other two. The piece includes a four-question checklist for whether an LLM call is really a decision: closed answer set, text input, latency matters, high volume.

Why it matters: This is the first independent reproduction in the decision-model space rather than vendor numbers - the exact evaluation angle the capstone tracks, and a template for how to run one (public datasets, list prices, client-side latency, no prompt tuning). Their stated caveats: one small chat model tested, Jev is hosted-only and text-only, and a valid typed answer is not the same as a correct one.

Why temperature 0 isn't deterministic in LLM inference

CraftInference

Daily Dose of DS explains why greedy decoding still varies run to run: GPU kernels are not batch-invariant, so floating-point reduction order changes with batch composition and kernel scheduling, and tiny numeric differences compound until they flip a token.

Why it matters: Practical consequence for evals and debugging: same prompt and same seed only reproduce under fixed batching and kernel configurations. When an eval flakes or a bug appears and vanishes, suspect batch-invariance before your data - and pin batch size and kernel settings when you need stable outputs.

Four decisions that make or break an agent harness

AgentsFrom production

Tejas Kumar (IBM) distills harness engineering into four ownership decisions: who decides the task is done, who holds the credentials, who says stop, and who is to blame when a run goes wrong. His answer for the first three: the harness, in code - the model proposes, the harness decides. The fourth is how you learn which of the two to fix. He also confesses his own demo harness could be talked into typing a Hacker News password.

Why it matters: This is the design counterpart to Saturday's benchmark finding: if harness choice barely moves scores, what remains is where a harness actually earns its keep - termination, credential scoping and kill-switch logic in deterministic code rather than in the prompt. The password anecdote is the security lesson: anything the model can persuade the harness to type is an exfiltration path.

uzu hits 92 tok/s on a base M5 by breaking the one-token-per-pass ceiling

InferenceCraft

Daily Dose of DS benchmarked the open-source uzu engine (Rust + Metal kernels, by Mirai Labs) running Qwen3.5 9B on a base M5 MacBook Pro: 92.1 tok/s vs 22.0 for llama.cpp and 25.1 for MLX. A 4-bit 9B model at ~153 GB/s memory bandwidth caps ordinary decoding near 29 tok/s; uzu averages ~7.5 accepted tokens per pass using tree speculative decoding - a DFlash drafter proposes 16 positions, a 56.7M-param Weaver adapter arranges candidates into a tree, and the 9B model verifies the whole tree in a single pass. It ships its own 4-bit checkpoints, runs verification on the GPU's int8 matrix path, and has Swift, Python, TypeScript and Rust bindings. The numbers are the newsletter's single-machine run, not an independent benchmark.

Why it matters: Local dev loops and on-device features live or die on tokens/sec, and this applies the server-side speculative decoding trick to Apple silicon - the acceptance-rate math (draft quality, tree width, verification batching) is the same thing you tune in a production serving stack. Check acceptance rates on your own prompts before trusting the 4x headline.

Why DeepSeek keeps its KV cache on disk - and serves 56% of input tokens from it

InferenceCraft

Daily Dose of DS walks through DeepSeek's open-sourced DeepInfer inference stack: Multi-head Latent Attention compresses the KV cache by ~93% versus DeepSeek's earlier 67B model, making it small enough that SSDs beat recompute, and the 3FS distributed file system reads cached prefixes from thousands of SSDs in parallel, streaming them to the GPU layer by layer while the model is already running. In production: a 128K-token cached prompt returns its first token in 500ms instead of 13s, 56% of daily input tokens are served from disk, and cached prefixes are retained 3 days for agent traffic vs 7 days for chat.

Why it matters: Prefix caching is the single biggest lever on agent workloads - your system prompt and tool definitions are re-sent on every call - and this shows the full design path: shrink the artifact (MLA), widen the storage (distributed SSD), overlap the transfer (layer-wise streaming). The 3-day vs 7-day retention split is also a hint about how they price agent vs chat traffic; steal that thinking for your own cache policies.

Jev beats Laya on 9 of 11 agent decision points - and the authors audit their own inflated claims

ResearchWeb

A paired evaluation (Laya AI / TypeSafe AI) tested open-weight Laya against hosted Jev on 11 agent decision points built from 18 public sources: 7,283 base cases plus 6,640 robustness variants, byte-identical inputs, cross-hardware and cross-day reproducibility checks. Jev is significantly more accurate on 9 of 11 decision points (+10.8 to +46.0 points); neither model beats chance on zero-shot model routing; they tie on RAG relevance gating. Laya flips 30% of its answers when the option order is reversed and drops to 31% accuracy with 50 similar candidate tools, where Jev holds 98% on items with a unique correct tool. The authors then audit their own pipeline and publish what they got wrong: an omitted pre-screen cost (23.9% claimed savings, 4.3% actual), gate accuracy reported as end-to-end quality (58% vs 98%), in-sample thresholds, and a 'channel effect' in injection testing. All cases, raw outputs and analysis code are public.

Why it matters: Two reasons this leads the week. First, the decision-model space now has a rigorous paired benchmark with reproducibility checks - the evidence standard the capstone asks for. Second, the self-audit is the rarest thing in applied evaluation work: a team showing how its own headline deployment claims were inflated, and by exactly how much. Steal both halves: the paired-test design and the audit checklist. Note the affiliation - this comes from Laya's own team, which makes the honesty more valuable, not less.

STAIR: IBM's retriever reads the table of contents instead of embedding chunks

Craft

IBM Research's STAIR (arXiv, early September - resurfaced this week via viral and partly wrong coverage) replaces chunk-and-embed retrieval with the document's own table of contents: a LoRA-fine-tuned Mistral 7B receives the query plus the book's full ToC and generates the single leaf section title that answers it, with decoding constrained to valid leaf nodes only. On SearchTome, their new 18-book, 6-domain benchmark, it reaches 82.6% Recall@1 vs 76.9% for a fine-tuned DSI baseline (statistically significant in all six domains), 68.7% DPR and 59.5% BM25. The viral '65x fewer hallucinations' headline is a narrow validity metric - 0.05% vs 3.25% invalid-section generations - not answer accuracy; end-to-end retrieval error improves about 1.3x (18.67% vs 24.31%).

Why it matters: If you build RAG over structured documents - textbooks, technical reports, anything with a real table of contents - structure-aware retrieval is a legitimate alternative to vector search, and the constrained-generation trick (the model can only emit real section titles) is reusable anywhere a model must pick from a closed set. The costs: one fine-tune per corpus, and documents need a clean ToC. All numbers are the paper's; SearchTome and the eval queries are public, so the claims are reproducible.

Synthetic data in 2026: what the research actually supports

CraftWeb

A research-grounded synthesis of when synthetic data works: four categories with different risk profiles - generated examples (QA pairs, instructions), rewritten real data, tasks and environments with graders for agent training, and synthetic stand-ins for people or private data. One standout: Apple used an LLM as a world model that simulates API responses and state changes to train API-calling agents, with a judge model filtering bad trajectories - no real environment needed.

Why it matters: The practical frame for your own data budget: synthetic data is cheapest where a grader can check the output (code, API calls, math), riskiest where it substitutes for human judgment, and 'rewritten real data' beats 'generated from nothing' when you have any seed corpus. If your evals or agent training runs ever stall on data access or privacy review, the tasks-plus-grader pattern is the one to steal.
